8-bit decimal display decoder

Binary-to-decimal conversion on a Cyclone V FPGA, driving three 7-segment displays

Complete
Platform
Intel DE1-SoC (Cyclone V)
Tools
Quartus Prime, LPM divider and comparator IP, VWF simulation
Dates
Sept – Oct 2026
Context
EECE 2160 Embedded Design, Lab 2, with a lab partner

ResultDisplays any 8-bit input (0–255) in decimal across three 7-segment displays, with leading zeros suppressed.

Goal

Display any 8-bit binary input (0–255) from the board's switches as a decimal number on three 7-segment displays.

What I did

  • Built a 16-row truth table and derived minimized Boolean expressions for all seven segments with Karnaugh maps, forcing every non-BCD input (10–15) to blank the display.
  • Packaged the decoder and an enable control into reusable blocks, then chained two divide-by-10 stages: the remainders become the ones and tens digits, and the final quotient becomes the hundreds digit.
  • Suppressed leading zeros with comparator blocks, enabling each display from the quotient rather than its own digit so a middle zero still shows (205 displays as 205, not 2 5).

Challenges and results

On the first hardware test, non-BCD inputs stayed lit. I traced it to the Karnaugh maps, where those inputs were treated as don't-cares instead of 1s, corrected the expressions, and confirmed the fix in simulation. The final design displayed 7, 19, and 173 correctly on the board.

Biomimetic heated jacket

Wearable heating system inspired by penguin thermoregulation

Complete
Platform
Arduino
Hardware
Power relays, heating pads, temperature sensors
Dates
Spring 2025
Context
GE 1502 Cornerstone of Engineering 2, biomimicry final
Team & role
Team of 4. I co-led the wiring and final installation.

ResultA working heated jacket that switches heat based on wrist temperature readings, built over three prototypes.

Goal

Penguins survive extreme cold by redirecting blood flow to control where heat is kept. We set out to mimic that with a jacket that senses temperature and sends heat where the body needs it most.

What I did

  • Mapped heating-pad placement to the areas of the torso most critical for heat retention.
  • Integrated an Arduino, power relays, heating pads, and wrist-mounted temperature sensors, and wrote the control code that switches heat based on sensor readings.
  • Wired, soldered, and troubleshot the system across three prototypes before the final build.

Challenges and results

An extra fabric layer wasted too much heat given our limited voltage, so we mounted the pads directly on the jacket. Our first heating pads turned out to be dead, so we bench-tested replacements on a DC supply before installing them. Next time, I'd sew the wiring into the fabric and add waterproofing.

Cattle EID tag

GPS-enabled ear tag for livestock tracking and record keeping

Prototype
Platform
SparkFun RedBoard Artemis Nano, GPS module
Language
C++ (Arduino)
Dates
Oct – Dec 2024
Context
GE 1501 Cornerstone of Engineering 1, team of 4
Shown at
First-Year Engineering Design Expo

ResultA working GPS ear-tag prototype that stores 24 hours of location data, built on a $40 budget and presented at the Design Expo.

Goal

New USDA rules require certain cattle to carry ear tags that can be read both visually and electronically. We designed a tag that goes further, adding GPS tracking and per-animal records to help ranchers manage herds and control disease.

What I did

  • Integrated a GPS module with the Artemis Nano over serial to track location in real time, storing up to 24 hours of location data.
  • Programmed a data system that ties each tag to the animal's location, name, age, and owner.
  • Helped design a 3D-printed case that houses the electronics inside a standard ear-tag shape.

Challenges and results

The $40 budget drove our part choices and a 3D-printed case instead of nylon, and we cut a planned QR-code interface to stay on schedule. A next version would use a wider-coverage GPS antenna and send data over Bluetooth instead of a wired serial link.
